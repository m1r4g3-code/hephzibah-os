# Value-Based Pricing Framework

The fundamental rule: price is a fraction of value, not a multiple of cost. Every price calculation starts with "what is the outcome worth?" not "how many hours will this take?"

---

## The Three Pricing Errors

**Error 1: Hourly thinking.**
If you estimate 80 hours at $50/hr = $4,000, you have priced the cost to build, not the value of what is built. The client does not care how long it takes. They care what it does for their business. A system that saves an executive 20 hours/week is worth $200k/year to their business whether it took 80 hours or 800 hours to build.

**Error 2: Fear of the number.**
The number that felt big when you quoted it is small relative to what the client gains. Fear of price communicates that you don't believe in the value. If you believe in the value, the number is obvious.

**Error 3: Anchoring to what you've been paid before.**
A $500 project in your history is not a reference point for a $50,000 project. The reference point for a $50,000 project is: what does the market charge for this scope, and what is this outcome worth?

---

## The Value Calculation Method

Run this calculation before naming any price on a deal over $5k.

### Step 1: Quantify the time saved

How many hours per week does the current manual process consume?

- Daily reporting compiled manually: 2-3 hours/week
- Weekly reporting: 4-8 hours/week
- Inbox management: 5-10 hours/week
- Board report preparation: 8-16 hours/month (2-4 hours/week)
- Meeting preparation: 3-5 hours/week
- Total for the NZ engagement: easily 20-30 hours/week across the group

### Step 2: Apply the opportunity cost rate

What is this person's time worth per hour?

- Senior executive, NZ engineering group: NZD $200-500/hr opportunity cost
- At NZD $200/hr, 20 hours/week = NZD $4,000/week saved
- Over one year: NZD $208,000 in time value recovered

### Step 3: Calculate the capacity unlock

Beyond time saved: what does this system allow that was not possible before?

- Managing 4 businesses instead of 2 without additional admin headcount
- Each additional business = additional revenue the group generates
- If each engineering business generates NZD $3M/year, unlocking one more business = NZD $3M in revenue capacity
- The system is worth a fraction of that capacity, not a fraction of the build cost

### Step 4: Price as a fraction of annual value

The standard consulting benchmark: 10-20% of first-year value.

- Annual time value recovered: NZD $208,000
- 10% = NZD $20,800 (USD ~$12,500) — floor price
- Revenue capacity unlocked (one additional business): NZD $3M
- 3% of that = NZD $90,000 (USD ~$55,000) — strategic price

**The correct price for the NZ Management Operating System engagement is between USD $35,000 (entry, Phase 1) and USD $100,000 (full engagement, all phases).** This is not a guess. This is value math.

## Market Reference Points (2026)

Use these as floor references. Never price below the market floor.

| Scope | Market price |
|---|---|
| Simple workflow automation (1-2 connected systems) | $1,000-$5,000 |
| Mid-complexity automation (3-5 systems, AI layer) | $5,000-$25,000 |
| Multi-system AI automation (5-8 workflows) | $25,000-$75,000 |
| Management operating system (multi-business, AI, reporting) | $50,000-$200,000 |
| Microsoft 365 enterprise integration (Graph API, Entra ID) | $15,000-$40,000 for integrations alone |
| Hourly rate, AI automation specialist | $75-$150/hr freelance, $125-$250/hr agency |

Source: jahanzaib.ai, evolvaiagents.com, agixtech.com, research 2026-08-10.

## The Three-Option Proposal Structure (Alan Weiss)

When presenting investment options, always offer three:

1. **Minimum:** Entry scope, lowest risk, immediately actionable. Priced at the floor.
2. **Recommended:** Full scope for the problem. This is what you actually recommend.
3. **Maximum:** Everything plus expansion, partnership, ongoing. Priced at the ceiling.

The three-option structure does two things:
- It shifts the client's mental frame from "do I hire this person?" to "which option do I choose?"
- It anchors the recommended option in the middle, making it feel like the rational choice

The maximum option is not expected to be chosen. Its job is to make the recommended option feel reasonable.

## How to Communicate Price Without Apologizing

The price is named once. Directly. Then the value case is made.

**Wrong:** "So I'm thinking... maybe around $35,000? But I'm flexible on that. We can discuss."

**Right:** "Phase 1 is $35,000. That covers the discovery sessions, all four business system integrations, the AI reporting engine, stability testing, and the handoff documentation. Based on what you've described, this system saves your team 15-20 hours of manual reporting per week. At conservative executive time value, you recover that investment inside 90 days."

State the number. State what it covers. State the value. Stop talking. Let them respond.

Silence after the number is not a problem. It is the client thinking. If you fill the silence with caveats and flexibility, you signal that you don't believe the number.

---

## When the Client Says "That's More Than I Expected"

Do not lower the price immediately. Ask one question first:

"What were you expecting?"

Listen to their answer. Then:

Option A — Their number is close: "I can work with that. Let me look at what adjustments make sense to the scope at that investment level." (Scope down, not price down.)

Option B — Their number is far apart: "I hear you. There's a real gap between where you were thinking and what this scope requires. Let me ask — is the gap about the overall budget, or is it about the Phase 1 entry point specifically? Because we can structure this differently." (Probe before conceding anything.)

Option C — They just need a moment: Say nothing. The silence is not rejection. It is processing. The person who speaks first after naming a price often ends up negotiating against themselves.

**The rule: never lower the price before understanding what drove the objection.**

---

## The Pricing Conversation the OS Always Runs Internally

Before any price is output:
1. What is the outcome worth over one year? (Value calculation)
2. What does the market charge for this scope? (Market reference)
3. What is the entry price that makes Phase 2 feel obvious? (Phase structure)
4. What is the price that reflects what this actually takes to build? (Realistic scoping)
5. Is the price I'm about to name something I can defend with value math?

If the answer to question 5 is no, recalculate. Do not output a price you cannot defend.
