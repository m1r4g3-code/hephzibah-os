# Executive Presence and High-Value Deal Framework

This node consolidates four external frameworks into one operational reference for deals over $10k. These frameworks were developed by the world's highest-earning consultants. Applied to freelance, they produce the results that gap ordinary freelancers from those who close $50k-$200k engagements.

---

## Framework 1: Alan Weiss — Conceptual Agreement Before Proposals

**The principle:** A proposal is a written confirmation of an agreement that already exists. Never write a proposal before getting verbal agreement on three things from the economic buyer:
1. Objectives (what outcomes the engagement must produce)
2. Measures of success (how both parties will know it worked)
3. Value to the organization (what it's worth to them if it succeeds)

Weiss's rule: "A proposal is a written statement of the conceptual agreement already gained in prior discussions. If this has not been gained, a proposal is little better than a spin of the roulette wheel." Proposals with prior conceptual agreement close at 80%+. Proposals without it close at under 20%.

**Application to freelance:** Before writing any proposal or SOW over $10k, have a discovery call. On the call, get explicit verbal agreement on: what success looks like, how we'll measure it, and what it's worth to the business. Then write the proposal as a confirmation, not a pitch.

**The Nine-Component Proposal (Weiss structure):**
1. Situation Appraisal — the shared context
2. Objectives — desired outcomes (not deliverables — outcomes)
3. Metrics — how progress and success will be measured
4. Value — organizational impact in the client's language ("saves $200K/year in reporting time")
5. Methodology and Options — three escalating ways to meet objectives
6. Timing — elapsed time per option
7. Joint Accountabilities — what each party provides
8. Credentials — brief citation of relevant experience
9. Terms and Acceptance — fees, sign-off

**The three-option structure:** Option 1 meets all objectives at base. Option 2 adds speed or depth. Option 3 maximizes outcomes and impact. All three options meet all stated objectives — they are different value configurations, not different scopes. This shifts the client from "do I hire Emmanuel?" to "which option do I want?"

## Framework 2: The Challenger Sale — Commercial Insight

**The principle:** 57% of B2B buying decisions are made before the first vendor conversation. By the time you get on a call, the client has already self-diagnosed. Classic solution selling — "ask about their needs, map your features to their needs" — doesn't work for complex deals because the client thinks they already know what they need. What closes complex deals is **Commercial Insight**: teaching the client something new about their own business.

**The sequence:**
1. Warm up — frame the context they recognize
2. Reframe — challenge one assumption they currently hold about their own situation
3. Rational drowning — quantify the cost of staying the same
4. Emotional impact — make it personal (their career, their team, their stress)
5. New way forward — a different approach they haven't considered
6. Your solution — now connected to their changed understanding

**What commercial insight is not:** It is not product education. It is not telling them what you do. It is a new perspective on the client's problem that they didn't have before talking to you.

**What it looks like in practice (NZ engineering example):**
"Most business group leaders at your stage build separate reporting for each business and then try to consolidate manually at the top. The problem is that by the time the consolidated picture arrives, it's already 2-3 days stale. The decisions you make on Monday are based on Thursday's data. I'd want to understand: how many decisions have you made this month that you later found out were based on outdated numbers?"

This reframes the problem from "I need automation" to "I'm making decisions on stale data." That reframe positions the system differently and justifies a different price.

**How to develop commercial insight:**
Study the industry's economics. Know the margins, cost structures, failure modes. Read trade publications. Find the thing practitioners almost universally get wrong — the conventional wisdom that is costing them money. Build the engagement around correcting that assumption.

## Framework 3: MEDDPICC — Qualification for Deals Over $10k

Run this scorecard mentally on every deal before investing time in a proposal. If score is too low, qualify out or close the gaps before writing.

| Letter | Meaning | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| M — Metrics | Quantified business impact | "What does success look like in numbers? How are you currently measuring this?" |
| E — Economic Buyer | The person who can write the check without approval | "Who makes the final call on this? Have I spoken to them directly?" |
| D — Decision Criteria | What standards will the solution be evaluated against | "What does a successful solution need to do for you to feel confident recommending it?" |
| D — Decision Process | The sequence from interest to signed contract | "Who else needs to be involved? What does the approval process look like?" |
| P — Paper Process | Legal, security review, procurement, NDA, vendor onboarding | "Once we agree on scope, how long does it take to get a contract in place?" |
| I — Identify Pain | Specific problem with time-sensitive cost | "What happens if you don't solve this in the next 6 months?" |
| C — Champion | Internal advocate who will fight for you in rooms you're not in | "Could you set up a 20-minute call with [economic buyer]?" (If they won't, they're not a real champion) |
| C — Competition | Who else is being evaluated and on what criteria | "Are you looking at other approaches to solving this?" |

**The Economic Buyer rule:** If you have never spoken directly to the person who signs the check, you do not have a qualified deal. You have a relationship with their gatekeeper.

**The Paper Process rule:** On deals over $25k, ask about the paper process (legal, security review, NDA requirements) in the first conversation. This is where deals go to die at the 11th hour. Ask early: "Once we agree on scope, what's the process to get a contract in place?"

**Qualifying out:** If the client has no pain with urgency, no economic buyer accessible, and no clear decision process — the deal will not close regardless of how good the proposal is. Qualifying out early saves time and protects energy for real opportunities.

## Framework 4: BLUF and "So What" — Executive Communication

**The core shift:** Executives do not want to understand how something works. They want to know what decision to make. Technical communicators build to conclusions. Executives stop reading before they get there.

**BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front:**
Always lead with the answer, not the reasoning.

Wrong: "We analyzed three different integration approaches, considered the tradeoffs around reliability and cost, and ultimately concluded that..."

Right: "The integration will use the Microsoft Graph API. Here is why that is the right choice for your environment."

State the answer. Then explain. Never the reverse.

**The "So What" Test (3 steps):**
1. What — state the fact or finding
2. So What — explain why it matters to their specific business
3. Now What — give a specific next step

Example: "Your current reporting cycle takes 3 days from data collection to delivery [What]. By the time you see last week's numbers, you've already made 15 decisions on the week's data that may need to be revisited [So What]. Phase 1 gets you same-day reporting. That means you start Monday with Friday's actual numbers, not last Tuesday's [Now What]."

**Four rules for executive communication:**
1. Speak in outcomes, not outputs. Not "I built a data pipeline" — "Your team spends zero hours on data collection now."
2. Quantify everything. Specific numbers create credibility. "Saves time" is nothing. "Saves 23 hours per week" is a fact they can act on.
3. Name the risk. Frame the cost of inaction. "If this isn't solved, what does that mean for the next business you add to the group?"
4. One specific next step. Never "let me know what you think." Always "Does Thursday at 10AM work to review the scope together?"

## Framework 5: ROI Business Case (Deals Over $15k)

Build a one-page business case alongside every SOW for deals over $15k. This is not a spreadsheet. It is a clear, three-column summary.

**The master formula:**
ROI = (Labor Cost Reduction + Error Reduction + Revenue Uplift + Capacity Unlocked) ÷ (Engagement Fee + Infrastructure + Client Time Investment)

**The five value categories:**
1. **Time savings** — hours/week eliminated × fully loaded hourly rate (salary + 25-40% overhead). This is almost always the largest number.
2. **Error reduction** — current cost of rework × elimination percentage.
3. **Speed-to-value** — how many days faster does something happen? Each day has a dollar value (revenue delayed = revenue at risk).
4. **Capacity unlocked** — headcount growth avoided. If automation handles work that would have required a new hire at $60K/year, that's $60K annually in the numerator.
5. **Competitive protection** — harder to quantify, but frame as risk of inaction: "Competitors who automate this in the next 18 months will compress your margins."

**Industry benchmarks (automation engagements):**
- Document and reporting automation: 150-300% three-year ROI, 6-10 month payback
- AI workflow automation: 20-40% first-year ROI, 200-400% second-year as costs amortize
- Typical payback period for mid-market automation: 8-18 months

**How to present it on the call:**
"At your current scale, this engagement pays for itself in [X] months and frees [Y] hours/week permanently after that. Here's the math." Show three numbers: current cost, engagement fee, months to payback. That is the entire case. The CFO in the room needs nothing more.

## The Rapid Industry Intelligence Process

Used by strategy consultants (McKinsey, Bain) to enter any industry in 48 hours with enough credibility to hold a senior conversation.

**Step 1 (Hours 1-4): Read Risk Factors and MD&A from two competitors' annual reports**
These sections reveal the industry's actual economic pressures. Written by executives for investors. No marketing. Pure business reality.

**Step 2 (Hours 4-8): Map Porter's Five Forces**
Competitive rivalry, new entrant threat, substitute threat, supplier power, buyer power. This explains where margins are made and lost in the sector.

**Step 3 (Hours 8-16): Trace the value chain**
Map every step from raw input to end customer. Every handoff is a potential pain point. Every coordination failure is an automation opportunity.

**Step 4 (Hours 16-24): PESTEL scan**
Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal. What external forces are creating urgency in this industry right now?

**Step 5 (Hours 24-48): Trade publication scan**
Read the last 3 months of the dominant trade publication. Know the current anxieties. Use the vocabulary. The fastest credibility move: quote something from their current industry discourse in the first meeting.

**The T-shaped principle:** Deep expertise in automation (the vertical bar) combined with horizontal literacy across adjacent industries (the horizontal bar). Horizontal breadth is what allows rapid entry into any new client sector by applying established frameworks to new domains.

## Applying This to the Next $15k+ Deal

Before the proposal:
1. Run MEDDPICC — is the economic buyer accessible? Is there pain with urgency?
2. Run industry intelligence — what business model? What metrics drive their decisions?
3. Run technical feasibility — what is the biggest unknown?
4. Get conceptual agreement on call — objectives, measures, value
5. Develop one commercial insight — what do they currently believe that is costing them money?

On the call:
1. Open with BLUF — bottom line first
2. Use "So What" — fact, implication, next step
3. Teach the commercial insight — reframe before pitching
4. Name the single biggest unknown — signals you've thought deeper than anyone else
5. End with one specific next step — never "let me know what you think"

In the proposal:
1. Use Weiss's nine-component structure
2. Three investment options — choice of yeses
3. Include the ROI business case
4. Payment: 40% upfront / 30% midpoint / 30% completion
