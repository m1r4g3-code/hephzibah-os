## Core thesis

Charlie Munger's idea of "elementary worldly wisdom": most good thinking doesn't come from
raw intelligence, it comes from carrying a wide "latticework" of concepts (mental models)
borrowed from many disciplines — physics, biology, economics, statistics, game theory — and
matching the right one to the situation in front of you. A small number of these models
("super models") are broadly useful outside the field they came from. Learning ~300 of them
across nine themed chapters is meant to let you skip lower-level trial-and-error thinking and
jump straight to higher-level pattern recognition — "super thinking."

## Catalog by category

### Avoiding bad reasoning (Ch.1–2)
- **First principles / de-risking** — break a plan down to its actual load-bearing
  assumptions, then test the riskiest ones cheaply before building further, instead of
  polishing on top of an unverified assumption.
- **Ockham's razor / Hanlon's razor** — prefer the simplest explanation for an event; assume
  carelessness over malice when someone's behavior seems bad, since it's more often true.
- **Confirmation bias / backfire effect / thinking gray / Devil's advocate** — you dig in
  harder on a belief when confronted with disproving facts; the fix is deliberately delaying
  judgment and forcing yourself to argue the other side before deciding.
- **Fundamental attribution error / self-serving bias** — you explain others' bad behavior by
  their character, and your own by circumstance; flip the lens to judge people fairly.
- **Root cause analysis (5 Whys)** — the first cause you find (proximate cause) is rarely the
  real one; keep asking "why" until you hit the structural cause you can actually fix.
- **Goodhart's law / cobra effect / Streisand effect** — the moment a measure becomes a
  target, people optimize the measure instead of the underlying goal, often making things
  worse than doing nothing.
- **Tragedy of the commons / free rider problem / moral hazard** — shared resources get
  depleted when individually-rational choices ignore the cost each choice imposes on everyone
  else; more protection lowers your own caution.
- **Technical debt / path dependence / preserving optionality / precautionary principle** —
  short-term shortcuts constrain your future options; weigh whether a decision is reversible
  (move fast) or irreversible (slow down and protect optionality).

### Time and effort (Ch.3)
- **Opportunity cost / BATNA** — every choice costs you the best thing you didn't pick; know
  your real alternatives before valuing any option (BATNA is the negotiation-specific case —
  already in your standing framework).
- **Pareto principle (80/20) / law of diminishing returns** — a small slice of effort usually
  drives most of the result; find it, and know when additional polish stops paying off.
- **Sunk-cost fallacy / loss aversion / Concorde fallacy** — money or time already spent is
  gone regardless of what you do next; deciding to continue should be based only on future
  value, not on what you've already put in.
- **Eisenhower Decision Matrix / present bias / commitment devices / default effect** —
  urgent-but-unimportant work crowds out important-but-not-urgent work unless you protect it
  deliberately; use binding commitments and defaults to beat short-term temptation.

### Adaptation and change (Ch.4)
- **Inertia / strategy tax / Shirky principle** — long-held positions and institutions resist
  change even when the change is obviously better; an institution will fight to preserve the
  problem it exists to solve.
- **Critical mass / tipping point / technology adoption life cycle (innovators → early
  adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards) / network effects** — ideas and
  products don't grow linearly; they need a threshold of adopters before they take off, and
  each new node in a network makes the network more valuable to everyone already in it.
- **Activation energy / catalyst / forcing function** — the hardest part of change is the
  first push; find something that lowers that initial cost (a catalyst) or build a recurring
  event that forces the change to keep happening (a forcing function).
- **Luck surface area** — deliberately increasing the number and diversity of situations you
  put yourself in increases the rate at which "lucky" breaks find you.

### Reading data (Ch.5)
- **Correlation ≠ causation / confounding factors** — two things moving together doesn't mean
  one causes the other; look for a third factor driving both.
- **Selection bias / survivorship bias / nonresponse bias** — conclusions drawn from whoever
  happened to stick around, respond, or succeed systematically miss the people who didn't,
  and that missing group is often the more informative one.
- **Gambler's fallacy / regression to the mean** — a streak doesn't make the next outcome more
  likely to reverse; and any unusually good or bad result tends to be followed by something
  closer to average, for purely statistical reasons, not narrative ones ("sophomore slump").
- **Law of small numbers** — a small sample can look like a strong signal and still be noise;
  don't overreact to a handful of data points.

### Making decisions under uncertainty (Ch.6)
- **Cost-benefit analysis with discounting** — go beyond a plain pro/con list: assign rough
  dollar (or scored) values including intangibles, and discount future costs/benefits back to
  today's value before comparing options.
- **Decision trees / sensitivity analysis** — map out branching probabilities and payoffs
  explicitly rather than holding them fuzzily in your head; then test which input assumptions
  actually move the final answer, so you know where more research is worth the time.
- **Black swans / unknown unknowns / systems thinking / scenario analysis** — some risks are
  unknowable in advance; the response isn't better prediction, it's building in enough slack
  and diversity of scenario planning that a surprise doesn't sink you.
- **Groupthink** — groups converge on comfortable consensus and suppress dissent; actively
  seek disconfirming views before committing a group to a decision.

### Conflict and persuasion (Ch.7)
- **Game theory core (prisoner's dilemma, Nash equilibrium, iterated games, tit-for-tat)** —
  in one-shot adversarial situations the individually-rational move can trap both sides in a
  worse outcome than cooperating; in *repeated* interactions (the normal case with a client or
  partner) starting cooperative and mirroring the other side's last move outperforms constant
  betrayal or constant trust.
- **Cialdini's six influence weapons: reciprocity, commitment, liking, social proof, scarcity,
  authority** — the standard, empirically tested levers people use (and that get used on you)
  to move a decision. A different toolkit from Voss's tactical-empathy playbook — this is
  about the psychological triggers behind *why* a technique works, Voss is about *what to
  say* in the room.
- **Social norms vs. market norms / distributive vs. procedural justice / ultimatum game** —
  introducing money into a relationship governed by goodwill (social norms) can permanently
  destroy that goodwill even after the money is removed; people will sacrifice their own
  payout to punish a deal they perceive as unfair, so *perceived* fairness of process matters
  as much as the number itself.
- **Deterrence / containment / MAD (mutually assured destruction) / carrot-and-stick** —
  conflict avoidance tools: make bad behavior credibly costly (deterrence), stop a problem
  from spreading rather than trying to fully resolve it immediately (containment), and pair
  a real threat with a real reward.
- **Domino effect / slippery slope / broken windows** — cascading-consequence arguments are
  invoked far more often than they're actually true; treat them as a hypothesis to check, not
  a given.

### Building and leading teams (Ch.8)
- **Joy's law ("most of the smartest people work for someone else")** — you will rarely have
  the most talented people available; the win comes from arranging the people you *do* have
  so each one becomes a 10x contributor in their specific role, not from chasing star hires.
- **DRI (directly responsible individual) / Peter principle** — every deliverable needs one
  clearly named owner; don't promote someone into a role that requires different skills than
  the ones that made them succeed in their last role.
- **Radical candor / consequence-conviction matrix / deliberate practice** — effective
  feedback cares personally *and* challenges directly; coach hardest on high-consequence,
  high-conviction gaps; skills compound fastest through deliberate practice at the edge of
  ability, not repetition of what's already comfortable.
- **Impostor syndrome / Dunning-Kruger effect** — competent people underrate themselves right
  when they're learning fastest; novices overrate themselves right when they know the least —
  expect both and don't fully trust your own confidence level as a signal.

### Market power and competitive strategy (Ch.9)
- **Secrets / consensus-contrarian matrix** — outsized returns require being both right *and*
  contrarian; being right when everyone else already agrees only gets you an average outcome.
  A secret is something true and valuable that most people don't yet believe.
- **"Why now?"** — Sequoia Capital's standing question for any new venture: what changed
  recently that makes this the right time, when the same idea might have failed five years
  ago? If you can't answer it, be suspicious of the timing.
- **Moats: IP, hard-to-replicate process, exclusive relationships/data, brand, distribution
  control, unique team, network effects, pace of innovation** — sustainable advantage rarely
  comes from one source; the strongest positions stack several of these so they reinforce each
  other (Kodak's century-long dominance stacked nearly all of them at once — and still lost to
  digital because none of them were about the actual underlying technology shift).
- **Switching costs / lock-in / barriers to entry and exit** — the mechanisms that make
  customers, employees, or partners "stuck" (cancellation friction, learning curves, trusted
  relationships, network effects) are a direct, buildable source of defensibility.
- **Crossing the chasm / disruption** — the gap between early adopters and the mainstream
  market is the point where most promising ideas actually die; incumbents get disrupted by
  offerings that look worse on the metrics they currently compete on but open up a new
  dimension entirely.

## Highest-leverage models for Emmanuel

1. **First principles + de-risking (Ch.1).** Directly extends what he's already doing with
   `[[builds-before-asking]]` — instead of building a full workflow on spec and hoping it
   lands, this gives him a concrete method: name the assumption that would kill the deal if
   false ("this prospect actually has this pain," "this niche will pay $1.5–3k for this"), and
   test *that specific thing* cheaply before sinking more hours in. Useful at the very top of
   the outreach funnel, before the free-walkthrough-video-style mistake repeats itself.

2. **Cost-benefit analysis with discounting + sensitivity analysis (Ch.6).** He's making
   real financial decisions on thin margins (MacBook purchase, Miva fee deferral, whether a
   client project is worth the 40/30/30 structure). This gives him a repeatable way to put
   rough dollar values — including intangible ones like reputation or relationship cost — on
   a decision instead of going on gut feel, and to know which input (price, timeline, scope)
   actually swings the answer so he knows what to negotiate hardest on.

3. **Sunk-cost fallacy / loss aversion (Ch.3).** Directly relevant to the Giovanni/SERAMAN
   pattern already logged in memory — pipeline likely reused for new scope without renegotiation.
   The model gives language for the trap: effort already spent on a project is not a reason to
   keep discounting for it, and holding on to an underpriced arrangement "because we've come
   this far" is the exact fallacy to name and reject before the M2 renegotiation.

4. **Cialdini's six influence weapons (Ch.7).** A genuinely new, separate toolkit from Voss —
   Voss is what to say once you're in a live negotiation; Cialdini is what makes a cold
   message, proposal, or offer land *before* that conversation even starts. Reciprocity
   (leading with something free and specific, which is already his instinct with
   `[[builds-before-asking]]`), social proof (case studies, count of workflows shipped), and
   scarcity (real availability constraints, not fake ones) map cleanly onto outreach copy and
   proposal structure.

5. **Social norms vs. market norms / distributive vs. procedural justice (Ch.7).** Sharp tool
   for the Oba 50/50 partnership and any future partner splits: once money is explicitly
   introduced into what was a goodwill/social arrangement, it doesn't go back to goodwill even
   if the money is later removed. Also explains why *how* a price or split is arrived at
   (procedural justice) can matter to a counterpart as much as the number itself — relevant
   to any client who feels a quote is "unfair" even when it's objectively fair.

6. **Moats: switching costs and lock-in (Ch.9).** Directly feeds the startup thesis (memory
   system → digital twin). One-off automation builds have almost no moat — they're copyable
   and the client can walk after delivery. The model is a checklist for turning single
   projects into retained relationships: what creates real switching cost (data accumulated
   in the system, workflow dependency, a maintenance relationship) versus what's just a nice
   one-time deliverable.

7. **Goodhart's law (Ch.2).** Directly applicable to the 6-month discipline lock-in already
   running (16 tracked daily/weekly items). The moment any one of those 16 becomes "the
   number to hit" rather than a proxy for the underlying goal (actual discipline, actual
   fitness), there's a real risk of gaming the metric — logging compliance without the
   substance behind it. Worth a periodic gut-check on the log itself, not just the streak.

8. **Regression to the mean / law of small numbers (Ch.5).** Useful specifically for reading
   his own outreach numbers. A great week or a dead week of cold outreach is very likely noise
   at his current volume (10 messages/weekday), not a signal that the script, niche, or
   approach broke or suddenly started working. Prevents overreacting — abandoning a script
   after one bad week, or over-committing to one after one good week — before there's enough
   volume for the result to mean anything.
